# Executive AI Initiative Project

## Project purpose

This individual project turns the course content, discussions, and cases into a practical executive proposal for one AI initiative in the participant's institution.

The participant is not expected to design or build the technology. The participant is expected to show the judgment required to:

- identify a meaningful institutional need;
- decide whether AI is an appropriate response;
- describe how work and decisions would change;
- ask the right business, technical, data, risk, and adoption questions;
- define a small, testable pilot;
- establish ownership, measures, and decision gates;
- track the initiative after approval; and
- decide whether to stop, redesign, continue, or scale it.

The project should answer one governing question:

> Should our institution invest in this AI initiative, under what conditions, and how will we know whether it is creating value responsibly?

The project reinforces the course progression:

```text
UNDERSTAND -> IMAGINE -> DESIGN -> LEAD

Problem      Opportunity   System      Decision and
and limits   and value     and controls ongoing oversight
```

The standard throughout the project is:

> Possible is not the same as reliable. Reliable is not the same as valuable.

---

## Expected learning outcomes

By completing the project, participants should be able to:

1. Frame an AI initiative as a business and organizational change, not as a technology purchase.
2. Connect a use case to strategy, users, workflow, and measurable outcomes.
3. Distinguish GenAI, traditional machine learning, automation, and conventional software as possible responses.
4. Explain an AI system at an executive level: model, context, data, tools, integrations, user experience, controls, and monitoring.
5. Identify where human judgment and accountability must remain.
6. Define a pilot that tests the riskiest assumptions before major investment.
7. Evaluate value, feasibility, desirability, risk, and organizational readiness.
8. Govern a portfolio of AI initiatives using evidence and explicit decision gates.

---

## The assignment

Choose one real process, decision, service, product, or institutional capability that could be materially improved.

Good candidates are frequent enough to matter, have identifiable users, produce observable outcomes, and contain work that AI may assist with: understanding, extracting, searching, generating, predicting, recommending, reasoning, conversing, or acting.

The chosen case may involve employees, customers, citizens, students, patients, suppliers, or other stakeholders. Sensitive cases are acceptable only if they can be discussed without exposing confidential or personal information.

### Project hypothesis

Complete this sentence early and revise it throughout the course:

> We believe that using **[AI capability]** to help **[specific users]** perform **[specific task or decision]** will improve **[business outcome]** from **[baseline]** to **[target]**, while keeping **[principal risks]** within **[defined limits]**.

### Scope rule

Choose one bounded workflow. Do not propose "an AI strategy for the whole organization" or "a chatbot for everything."

A useful project has:

- one accountable executive sponsor;
- one primary user group;
- one workflow or decision;
- one leading operational measure;
- one business outcome measure; and
- one pilot that can produce evidence within approximately 90 days.

# Final Deliverable

Submit one concise **Executive AI Initiative Dossier** plus a short decision presentation.

## A. Executive AI Initiative Dossier

Recommended length: **6 to 10 pages**, excluding appendices.

The dossier should use the template below. It must be understandable to a mixed investment committee that includes business, technology, data, security, legal/compliance, finance, and people/change perspectives.

## B. Decision presentation

Recommended format: **5 slides, 7 minutes, followed by questions**.

1. Need, users, and strategic importance.
2. Current workflow and proposed AI-enabled workflow.
3. Value hypothesis, measures, and expected economics.
4. System concept, risks, safeguards, and human accountability.
5. Ninety-day pilot, stage-gate request, and decision required.

## C. One-page initiative card

The initiative card is the artifact an executive can use in a portfolio review after the course. It contains:

- project name and owner;
- problem and target users;
- strategic objective;
- current stage and decision date;
- value hypothesis and two or three KPIs;
- top three assumptions;
- top three risks and controls;
- funding and people committed;
- current status: green, amber, or red;
- next decision: stop, redesign, continue, or scale.

## 11. Ninety-day pilot charter

The pilot is an evidence-producing project, not a small production rollout.

### Hypotheses to test

1. **Desirability:** Users will adopt the redesigned workflow because it solves a meaningful problem.
2. **Feasibility:** The system can meet defined quality, integration, security, latency, and cost thresholds.
3. **Viability:** The expected institutional benefit justifies lifecycle cost and change effort.
4. **Responsibility:** Risks can be kept within the institution's tolerance with workable controls.

# Stage Gates

## Gate 0 - Admit to discovery

Decision: Is the problem important enough to investigate?

Required evidence:

- named owner and strategic link;
- affected users and workflow;
- credible problem evidence;
- initial alternatives and risk screen; and
- a time-boxed discovery request.

## Gate 1 - Approve a pilot

Decision: Is there enough evidence to test the initiative with real users and data?

Required evidence:

- baseline and value hypothesis;
- target workflow and user validation;
- technical concept and data access path;
- risk tier, legal basis, safeguards, and human oversight;
- evaluation set, thresholds, budget, and rollback plan; and
- explicit pilot boundaries.

## Gate 2 - Approve limited operation

Decision: Can the institution depend on the system in a bounded setting?

Required evidence:

- pilot results against pre-agreed thresholds;
- user adoption and workflow evidence;
- security, privacy, compliance, and accessibility approval;
- operating owner, monitoring, support, and incident response;
- validated unit economics; and
- unresolved risks with accepted owners and deadlines.

## Gate 3 - Approve scale

Decision: Is expansion justified and operationally sustainable?

Required evidence:

- demonstrated business outcome, not only activity;
- reliable performance across relevant populations and conditions;
- capacity, vendor, integration, and cost plan;
- workforce and process redesign plan;
- independent challenge or assurance proportionate to risk; and
- next review and decommissioning triggers.

At every gate, the valid decisions are **stop, redesign, continue with conditions, or advance**.

# Executive Operating Rhythm

Executives should govern outcomes and decisions without becoming substitute project managers. The cadence below separates weak signals that deserve daily attention from decisions that need deeper periodic review.

## Daily: 10-minute signal check

For live or high-impact initiatives, review exceptions rather than every task:

- material incidents, harmful outputs, security events, or regulatory concerns;
- sudden deterioration in quality, service, cost, or user behavior;
- blocked decisions requiring executive authority;
- unexpected external communication or autonomous action; and
- whether a pause threshold has been reached.

Executive action: **ask, unblock, escalate, or pause**. Do not redesign the model in the status meeting.

## Weekly: initiative review

Ask the business and technical owners:

1. What did we learn this week that changed an assumption?
2. Are users obtaining the intended benefit in the real workflow?
3. Which quality, risk, cost, or adoption indicator moved materially?
4. What failed, and was the failure visible and recoverable?
5. What evidence is still needed for the next gate?
6. Is any scope, data access, autonomy, supplier, or regulatory condition changing?
7. What decision or resource is required from me?

## Monthly: portfolio review

Review all initiatives on one page:

| Initiative | Owner | Stage | Value evidence | AI quality | Risk/control | Adoption | Spend vs plan | Next gate | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | | | | | | | |

Portfolio questions:

- Are resources concentrated on the institution's highest-value domains?
- Are duplicate tools or use cases being created?
- Which pilots are producing learning but not value?
- Which projects should stop so people and money can move elsewhere?
- Which shared data, platform, evaluation, security, or skills investments unblock several projects?
- Are risks concentrated in one vendor, model, dataset, or business process?
- Is realized value visible in operational and financial reporting?

## Quarterly: strategic and governance review

- reassess the portfolio against institutional strategy and risk appetite;
- review regulation, incidents, stakeholder impact, and audit findings;
- confirm ownership and the AI system inventory;
- review vendor concentration, exit plans, and lifecycle costs;
- decide which capabilities to build centrally and which to distribute;
- revisit workforce implications and skills; and
- retire systems that no longer create sufficient value or control.

## Stop / pause triggers

Define these before launch. Examples include:

- a prohibited use or absent legal basis;
- severe harm, security compromise, or confidential-data exposure;
- failure below a critical safety or quality threshold;
- users cannot exercise required oversight;
- costs exceed the approved tolerance without corresponding value;
- material scope or autonomy expansion without review;
- repeated incidents without effective corrective action; or
- evidence that a simpler alternative is superior.

# Executive Checklist

## Envision

- [ ] We started from a measurable institutional need, not an available tool.
- [ ] The initiative supports a named strategic objective.
- [ ] The primary user and affected stakeholders are known.
- [ ] The current workflow and baseline are documented.
- [ ] We considered process change, rules, automation, and traditional ML.
- [ ] The reason AI is required is specific and defensible.
- [ ] The project is bounded enough to test.

## Design

- [ ] The future workflow describes both AI and human work.
- [ ] Decision rights and escalation paths are explicit.
- [ ] Required data, context, systems, and permissions are known.
- [ ] Data quality, provenance, representativeness, retention, and lawful use have owners.
- [ ] The technical concept includes integration, security, reliability, observability, and fallback.
- [ ] Build/buy/partner assumptions include vendor dependency and exit.
- [ ] The team has business, user, technical, data, security, risk, and change perspectives.

## Evaluate

- [ ] Success measures include business outcome, operational performance, AI quality, safety, adoption, and economics.
- [ ] Baselines and targets are defined before the pilot.
- [ ] A credible comparison or counterfactual is planned.
- [ ] Evaluation uses representative, difficult, edge, and adversarial cases.
- [ ] Acceptance and stop thresholds are agreed in advance.
- [ ] The full workflow is tested, including humans and integrations.
- [ ] Feedback, incidents, and failures become new test cases.

## Govern

- [ ] One executive is accountable for the outcome.
- [ ] Business, technical, data, evaluation, and risk owners are named.
- [ ] The system is recorded in the institution's AI inventory.
- [ ] Risk classification and applicable regulation are documented.
- [ ] Human oversight is meaningful, trained, and operationally possible.
- [ ] Affected people have appropriate transparency, recourse, and appeal.
- [ ] Monitoring, incident response, rollback, and decommissioning are funded.
- [ ] Material changes trigger re-evaluation and approval.

## Lead and track

- [ ] The pilot tests the hardest assumptions, not only the easiest demo.
- [ ] The dashboard reports evidence, exceptions, and decisions rather than activity alone.
- [ ] Value is measured after adoption and reflected in operating/financial results.
- [ ] Portfolio reviews stop weak initiatives and redirect resources.
- [ ] Scale requires demonstrated value, reliability, adoption, and control.
- [ ] The executive can explain what happens when the AI is wrong.

- **Insufficient:** broad idea without a measurable need, accountable owner, feasible pilot, or risk response.

# Peer Challenge Questions

Each participant should receive challenge from at least two peers. Reviewers should ask:

1. What evidence would cause you to stop this project?
2. Why is AI necessary, and what simpler alternative was rejected?
3. Whose work, rights, or outcomes change if this succeeds or fails?
4. Which assumption is both uncertain and capable of destroying the business case?
5. Is the proposed human review meaningful, or merely ceremonial?
6. Can quality and value be measured before and after launch?
7. What is missing from the technical concept that could block production?
8. What changes when use expands from 50 cases to 50,000?
9. Who is accountable when the system, user, or vendor makes a mistake?
10. Would you personally approve the pilot? Under what conditions?

# Research Basis and Comparable Frameworks

This project template synthesizes several established approaches rather than treating any single framework as a complete recipe.

1. **NIST AI Risk Management Framework and Playbook.** NIST organizes AI risk work into **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage**, emphasizes continuous lifecycle management, clear roles, an AI inventory, contextual risk analysis, measurement, and documented responses. This informed the governance, risk, evaluation, inventory, and stage-gate sections.  
   Sources: [NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/), [NIST AI RMF Playbook](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/playbook/), [NIST Generative AI Profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)

2. **OECD AI accountability principle.** The OECD calls for traceability and systematic risk management throughout the AI lifecycle, with accountability based on each actor's role and ability to act. This informed explicit owners, decision records, traceability, and lifecycle accountability.  
   Source: [OECD AI Principle on Accountability](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P9)

3. **ISO/IEC 42001.** The AI management-system standard uses a Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving AI management. This informed the recurring executive cadence and the requirement to monitor, review, improve, and retire systems.  
   Source: [ISO overview of AI management systems](https://www.iso.org/artificial-intelligence/ai-management-systems)

4. **Microsoft's business planning and BXT approach.** Microsoft's guidance compares use cases across business impact, technical feasibility, and user desirability, and recommends beginning with business problems, defining measures, and piloting the hardest steps. This informed the opportunity scorecard and four pilot hypotheses.  
   Sources: [Business plan for AI agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/cloud-adoption-framework/ai-agents/business-strategy-plan), [Business, Experience, Technology framework](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-cloud/dev/copilot/isv/business-envisioning)

5. **Google People + AI Guidebook.** The guidebook connects user needs and success definition with data and evaluation, mental models, explainability and trust, feedback and control, and graceful failure. This informed the user, adoption, human factors, feedback, and failure-response sections.  
   Source: [People + AI Guidebook](https://pair.withgoogle.com/guidebook-v2/chapters)

6. **UK guidance on AI procurement and public-sector use.** The guidance recommends a clear problem statement, openness to alternative solutions, early impact assessment, multidisciplinary teams, ongoing evaluation, lifecycle oversight, integration planning, and knowledge transfer. This informed the alternatives table, pilot controls, vendor questions, and lifecycle checklist.  
   Sources: [Guidelines for AI procurement](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/guidelines-for-ai-procurement/guidelines-for-ai-procurement), [AI Playbook for the UK Government](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-playbook-for-the-uk-government)

7. **EU AI Act.** The Act uses a risk-based approach and places obligations on providers and deployers depending on the system and use. High-risk requirements include risk management, data quality, logging, documentation, human oversight, robustness, cybersecurity, and accuracy; transparency duties also apply to specified uses. This informed the initial risk screen and the instruction to obtain domain-specific legal review.  
   Sources: [European Commission AI Act overview](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), [Navigating the AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/navigating-ai-act)

These sources are guidance, not a substitute for the institution's legal, regulatory, security, procurement, audit, or sector-specific requirements.
