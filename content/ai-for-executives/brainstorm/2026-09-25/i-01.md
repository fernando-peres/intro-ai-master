I’d structure this as an **“AI & Generative AI for Executives”** course rather than a technical AI course. The goal is not to teach executives how to build models, but to develop enough **technical intuition, vocabulary, and strategic judgment** to identify opportunities, challenge proposals, manage AI initiatives, and make investment decisions.

A good progression is:

**AI foundations → GenAI shift → capabilities → how GenAI systems work → business applications → building AI products → management/governance → executive practice.**

### Proposed syllabus

| Module | Topic                                      | Executive intuition to develop                                                                              |
| ------ | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1      | **AI: What It Is and Why It Matters**      | Understand AI without hype; distinguish automation, analytics, ML, GenAI, and agents                        |
| 2      | **From Machine Learning to Generative AI** | Understand what fundamentally changed and why GenAI enables new categories of products                      |
| 3      | **How Generative AI Works**                | Develop intuition for LLMs, tokens, embeddings, transformers, context, inference, and probabilistic outputs |
| 4      | **The Multimodal AI Landscape**            | Understand what AI can now do with text, images, audio, video, code, and structured data                    |
| 5      | **From Models to AI Systems**              | Understand that an LLM alone is not an enterprise solution: context, RAG, tools, memory, workflows, agents  |
| 6      | **AI Agents & Agentic Systems**            | Understand agents, tool use, reasoning loops, autonomy, orchestration, and human-in-the-loop                |
| 7      | **Finding High-Value AI Opportunities**    | Identify where AI creates value versus where conventional software/automation is better                     |
| 8      | **Building AI Products**                   | Understand prototype → evaluation → production → monitoring → improvement                                   |
| 9      | **Economics, Risk & Governance**           | Reason about cost, ROI, privacy, security, hallucinations, reliability, regulation, and organizational risk |
| 10     | **Managing AI Initiatives**                | Know what questions to ask teams/vendors and how to evaluate architecture, claims, demos, and results       |
| 11     | **AI Strategy & Organization**             | Decide build/buy/partner, platform vs use case, operating model, talent, data and capability investments    |
| 12     | **Executive AI Lab / Capstone**            | Turn a real business problem into an AI initiative and defend the proposal                                  |

The important design choice is that **Modules 1–4 build intuition, not mathematical depth**. For example, when explaining embeddings, executives don't need cosine-similarity equations. They need the intuition that information can be represented in a semantic space, which enables semantic search, recommendations, clustering and retrieval.

Likewise, I would explain the GenAI shift as an evolution:

> **Traditional software:** humans specify rules → computer executes rules
> **Machine learning:** humans specify objective + data → model learns patterns
> **Generative AI:** model learns broad representations → users specify intent through language/context
> **Agentic AI:** model + context + tools + workflow → system can pursue multi-step objectives

That mental model gives you a bridge into the modern architecture.

### A particularly important module: from LLM to AI system

I would spend significant time here because this is where executive understanding often breaks down.

A useful conceptual architecture is:

```text
                    AI APPLICATION
                         │
             ┌───────────┴───────────┐
             │                       │
          CONTEXT                 MODEL
             │                       │
     ┌───────┼────────┐              │
     │       │        │              │
   Data    Memory   Knowledge       LLM
     │                │
     └──── Retrieval / RAG
                         │
                       AGENT
                         │
                ┌────────┼─────────┐
                │        │         │
              APIs     Tools    Systems
                │        │         │
                └────────┼─────────┘
                         │
                    ACTION / OUTPUT
```

This lets you introduce **context engineering, RAG, tool calling, agents, memory, orchestration, evaluation and guardrails** without turning the class into an engineering course.

The executive takeaway should be:

> **The model is a component. The business value comes from the system built around the model.**

That distinction is extremely useful when executives evaluate vendors or internal proposals.

### Teach capabilities before use cases

Instead of immediately showing “AI in HR / AI in Finance / AI in Marketing,” I'd first teach executives to think in terms of **capabilities**.

For example:

| AI capability     | What it enables                                    |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Generate          | Create text, images, code, audio, video            |
| Understand        | Interpret documents, conversations, images, video  |
| Extract           | Turn unstructured information into structured data |
| Search / Retrieve | Find information semantically                      |
| Summarize         | Compress large amounts of information              |
| Transform         | Translate, rewrite, classify, restructure          |
| Predict           | Estimate outcomes from data                        |
| Recommend         | Rank/select relevant items                         |
| Reason            | Work through problems and constraints              |
| Converse          | Natural-language interfaces                        |
| Act               | Call APIs, databases and enterprise systems        |
| Automate          | Execute multi-step workflows                       |
| Personalize       | Adapt experiences to users/context                 |

Then ask:

**“Which of these capabilities could change one of your organization's workflows?”**

That produces much better executive thinking than starting with a catalog of ChatGPT examples.

### Theory → intuition → practice

I would make this the teaching pattern throughout the course.

For every concept:

**1. Theory — What is it?**

Explain the minimum conceptual foundation.

**2. Intuition — Why does it behave this way?**

For example, an LLM predicts tokens rather than retrieving an objectively correct answer from a database. That intuition explains both its extraordinary flexibility **and** hallucinations.

**3. Business implication — Why should an executive care?**

If outputs are probabilistic, traditional software QA alone isn't sufficient.

**4. Executive question — What should I ask?**

For example:

> “How are we evaluating the quality of this system before and after deployment?”

**5. Practice — Apply it to a real situation.**

Give them a proposed AI project and have them challenge the proposal.

This turns technical knowledge into **executive judgment**.

### The executive questions framework

By the end, I would expect every participant to be able to interrogate an AI initiative with roughly these questions:

1. **What business problem are we solving?**
2. **Why AI?** Why isn't conventional software/automation sufficient?
3. **What AI capability is required?**
4. **What data/context does the system need?**
5. **What model(s) are required?**
6. **What does the surrounding system look like?**
7. **What actions can the AI take?**
8. **Where does a human remain in control?**
9. **How do we measure quality?**
10. **What happens when the AI is wrong?**
11. **What are the security/privacy/compliance implications?**
12. **What does it cost at scale?**
13. **How will we monitor it in production?**
14. **What business KPI should change if this succeeds?**

That could actually become a one-page **Executive AI Project Canvas** used throughout the course.

### Capstone

Rather than an exam, I'd have each executive choose a real process from their organization and produce something like:

```text
BUSINESS PROBLEM
      ↓
CURRENT PROCESS
      ↓
AI OPPORTUNITY
      ↓
AI CAPABILITIES REQUIRED
      ↓
DATA + CONTEXT
      ↓
AI SYSTEM / ARCHITECTURE
      ↓
HUMAN + AI WORKFLOW
      ↓
RISKS / GUARDRAILS
      ↓
EVALUATION
      ↓
BUSINESS KPI / ROI
      ↓
90-DAY PILOT
```

They would then present it as if requesting funding from an investment committee.

That tests the capability that matters most: **not whether an executive can explain a Transformer, but whether they can recognize a credible AI opportunity, ask the right technical/business questions, and manage the initiative from idea to production.**

Given your goal, I'd probably make the course approximately **30% foundations/technology, 30% applications and system design intuition, and 40% executive decision-making + hands-on cases**. This avoids the common failure mode of “AI for Executives” courses becoming either a shallow collection of ChatGPT tricks or an overly technical ML lecture.
