I would make **industry cases a formal component of every class**, immediately before the discussion. For executives, cases are the bridge between abstract concepts and “what does this mean for my organization?”

I’d also avoid only showcasing spectacular success stories. Use cases that expose a **management lesson**: human oversight, evaluation, workflow redesign, data/context, scalability, or why a seemingly good AI idea may not be worth doing.

A strong recurring 2-hour format would be:

**Concepts (35–40 min) → Industry Cases (20–25 min) → Executive Discussion (20–25 min) → Individual Project (30–35 min).**

## Class 1 — What Changed with Generative AI?

**Question:** *Why is this different from the AI companies were already using?*

### Concepts

AI → ML → Deep Learning → Foundation Models → GenAI; training vs inference; LLM intuition; tokens; transformers at a conceptual level; probabilistic generation; hallucination; and why general-purpose models changed the economics of building AI applications.

### Industry case: [Morgan Stanley + Generative AI](https://openai.com/index/morgan-stanley/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is an excellent first case because it is understandable without technical knowledge.

Morgan Stanley integrated GPT-based capabilities into wealth-management workflows. One application allows financial advisors to query the firm's internal knowledge; another turns recorded meetings into notes and draft follow-ups. The company reports that more than 98% of advisor teams use its AI assistant. Importantly, advisors review AI-generated meeting outputs before finalizing them. ([OpenAI][1])

You can draw the architecture on the board:

```text
Before

Advisor → Search documents → Read → Interpret → Respond


With GenAI

Advisor → Natural-language question
               ↓
        Enterprise Knowledge
               ↓
              LLM
               ↓
         Draft / Answer
               ↓
             Advisor
```

**Executive lesson:** GenAI changes the **interface between people and organizational knowledge**. The interesting innovation isn't merely text generation.

And Morgan Stanley provides a second lesson you'll revisit in Class 4: they built systematic **evaluations (evals)** around accuracy, summarization, translation and retrieval rather than assuming that a convincing demo was production-ready. ([OpenAI][1])

### Discussion

Now ask:

> Why is this different from Google/search?

> What part is AI and what part is existing organizational knowledge?

> Why didn't Morgan Stanley simply train its own model on everything?

> Why is the financial advisor still important?

> Where could a similar pattern exist inside your organization?

### Project

**Identify a business problem/opportunity.**

# Class 2 — What Can AI Do?

**Question:** *How do I recognize an AI opportunity?*

Here I would deliberately use **very different industries**.

### Concepts

Teach capabilities rather than products:

**Understand → Extract → Search → Generate → Analyze → Recommend → Reason → Act**

Then introduce modalities:

**Text | Image | Audio | Video | Code | Structured Data**

And embeddings/semantic representations at an intuitive level.

### Case A — [GitHub Copilot](https://github.blog/news-insights/research/research-quantifying-github-copilots-impact-on-developer-productivity-and-happiness/?pStoreID=bizclubgold252525252525252525252525252525252F10002525252527252525255B0252525255D&utm_source=chatgpt.com): Code

This is a powerful case because the AI doesn't replace the developer—it changes how the developer produces software.

In a controlled experiment involving 95 professional developers, GitHub reported that the Copilot group completed a programming task about **55% faster** on average. It's a specific experimental task, so I would explicitly caution students against generalizing “55% productivity increase” to all software development. ([The GitHub Blog][2])

Capabilities:

```text
Understand intent
      ↓
Generate code
      ↓
Developer evaluates
      ↓
Modify / accept
```

### Case B — [Ascendum field-service AI](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/how-we-help-clients/adding-a-powerful-new-tool-to-the-field-mechanics-toolbelt-ai?utm_source=chatgpt.com): Industrial knowledge

This is particularly nice for a European executive audience: Ascendum is Portugal-based and operates heavy-equipment businesses across multiple countries.

Field technicians need to navigate large quantities of technical information to diagnose machinery problems. Ascendum worked on GenAI capabilities intended to help agents diagnose and resolve issues faster, after evaluating roughly 30 potential GenAI use cases. ([McKinsey & Company][3])

Now ask the class to stop thinking about “chatbots.”

The underlying capability is closer to:

```text
Technician problem
        ↓
Understand
        ↓
Retrieve technical knowledge
        ↓
Reason over information
        ↓
Recommend action
        ↓
Technician acts
```

### Optional mini-case — Siemens Industrial Copilot

[Siemens Industrial Copilot](https://press.siemens.com/global/en/pressrelease/siemens-and-microsoft-scale-industrial-ai?utm_source=chatgpt.com) uses GenAI in industrial engineering and operations, including automation-code generation and operational support. Siemens reported in 2024 that more than 100 companies were using its Industrial Copilot. ([Siemens Press][4])

This helps break the perception that:

> GenAI = ChatGPT = office workers writing text.

### Discussion

Give them the cases and ask:

> **Ignore the product. What AI capabilities are actually being used?**

Then:

> Could the same capability pattern apply to another industry?

For example:

```text
Industrial maintenance
       ↓
Retrieve + Reason + Recommend

Legal
       ↓
Retrieve + Reason + Draft

Healthcare administration
       ↓
Extract + Retrieve + Summarize

Sales
       ↓
Understand + Recommend + Generate
```

That's a very important executive intuition: **capabilities transfer across industries.**

### Project

Participants map their own opportunity:

**Problem → AI capabilities → modalities → new workflow → business outcome.**

# Class 3 — From LLM to AI System

**Question:** *Why isn't buying access to an LLM enough?*

This is where the cases should become more architecturally sophisticated.

### Case A — Return to Morgan Stanley

Now revisit the same case from a completely different perspective.

Class 1 asked:

> What became possible?

Class 3 asks:

> **What had to be built around the model?**

Their system involves enterprise documents, retrieval, model behavior, evaluations, security controls, workflow integration and human review. Morgan Stanley says its retrieval capability expanded from answering questions against roughly 7,000 to a corpus of 100,000 documents. ([OpenAI][1])

Draw:

```text
                    Advisor
                       │
                       ▼
                 AI Application
                       │
              ┌────────┴────────┐
              │                 │
          Enterprise            LLM
           Knowledge             │
              │                  │
           Retrieval ────────────┘
                       │
                     Answer
                       │
                 Human Review
```

Now concepts such as **RAG, context engineering and evals have a business reason for existing.**

### Case B — [KPN agentic customer care](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/how-we-help-clients/how-kpn-is-building-an-agentic-ai-engine-for-customer-care?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

KPN provides a useful transition from copilots toward agentic systems. Its work included analyzing large volumes of anonymized call and chat interactions, identifying high-impact customer needs, mapping processes, and evaluating opportunities based on value, complexity and feasibility. The design also considers where humans should remain involved, particularly in sensitive customer situations. ([McKinsey & Company][5])

Use this to introduce:

```text
CHATBOT
Question → Answer


COPILOT
Human → AI → Recommendation → Human acts


AGENT
Goal → AI → Tools → Action → Observe → Continue


AGENTIC SYSTEM
Human
  ↓
Agent
  ├── Knowledge
  ├── Memory
  ├── CRM
  ├── APIs
  ├── Other agents
  └── Business systems
        ↓
      Action
```

### Discussion

Give the executives the statement:

> **“We want to implement an AI agent for customer service.”**

And tell them:

**You are the executive committee. You have 15 minutes before approving €1M. What do you need to know?**

They should gradually discover questions about data, integrations, permissions, autonomy, evaluation, security, failure modes and human intervention.

That's far more effective than lecturing them about agent architecture.

### Project

**Design their conceptual AI system.**

Problem → capabilities → data/context → AI → tools/systems → actions → humans.

# Class 4 — From AI Demo to Business Transformation

**Question:** *How do I decide which AI initiatives deserve investment—and how do I manage them?*

This class should contain a mixture of **success + caution**.

### Case A — [ING: scaling GenAI in banking](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/financial-services/our-insights/deploying-ai-at-speed-and-scale-talking-with-ings-marnix-van-stiphout?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

ING is useful because it moves the conversation beyond a single proof of concept. By 2025, the bank described using GenAI across five critical areas, including customer service, software engineering, KYC and compliance. Its leadership emphasized scope, safety, speed and putting the right people onto the initiative. ([McKinsey & Company][6])

Executive lesson:

> The question evolves from **“Can AI do this?”** to **“Can our organization repeatedly turn AI opportunities into reliable production systems?”**

### Case B — Morgan Stanley's eval approach

Return one final time to Morgan Stanley—but now discuss **measurement and governance**.

They didn't just ask:

> Does the demo look impressive?

They evaluated translation, summarization, retrieval and other behaviors against expert expectations, and continued regression testing after deployment. ([OpenAI][1])

That lets you introduce:

```text
Demo
  ≠
Production

Technical capability
  ≠
Business value

Accuracy
  ≠
Reliability

AI adoption
  ≠
Transformation
```

### Case C — The intentionally bad AI investment

I would actually **invent a realistic case** rather than name a company:

> A company proposes spending €400k building a custom GenAI application that generates standard HR leave-request forms. The existing HR platform already generates those forms reliably.

Ask:

> Should this be an AI project at all?

This teaches one of the most important executive lessons:

**Knowing when *not* to use AI is an AI capability.**

A useful prioritization framework is:

```text
                     BUSINESS VALUE
                          HIGH
                           ↑
                           │
        PRIORITIZE        │       STRATEGIC BET
                           │
                           │
 LOW EFFORT ───────────────┼────────────── HIGH EFFORT
                           │
        QUICK WIN          │       DEPRIORITIZE
                           │
                           ↓
                          LOW
```

A similar value-versus-effort approach is used in OpenAI's enterprise use-case guidance. ([OpenAI CDN][7])

### Discussion

Now make them an **AI Investment Committee**.

Present 3–4 fictional proposals based on the real cases:

1. Knowledge assistant for 5,000 employees
2. Autonomous customer-service agent
3. AI-assisted industrial maintenance
4. AI-generated internal forms

Give them €2M.

They must decide **what they would investigate further**, not simply choose their favorite technology.

They should ask about:

**Value → Feasibility → Data → Risk → Evaluation → Integration → Adoption → Cost → ROI.**

Then immediately apply that thinking to their individual projects.

## This changes the overall pedagogical structure

I'd make the repeated rhythm visible to students from Day 1:

```text
          CONCEPT
             │
             ▼
      REAL INDUSTRY CASE
             │
             ▼
     EXECUTIVE DISCUSSION
             │
             ▼
       "SO WHAT FOR US?"
             │
             ▼
     INDIVIDUAL PROJECT
```

That is stronger than **lecture → exercise** because the case becomes the cognitive bridge between theory and their organization.

And I'd deliberately **reuse Morgan Stanley across Classes 1, 3 and 4**. That's pedagogically useful rather than repetitive. In Class 1 they see **capability**, in Class 3 **architecture**, and in Class 4 **evaluation/governance**. They gradually realize that the same AI initiative can be examined through **technology, product, organizational, risk and investment lenses**.

For an executive course, I'd also avoid presenting 20 superficial cases. **6–8 cases studied deeply**—Morgan Stanley, GitHub, Ascendum, Siemens, KPN, ING plus one or two failure/poor-investment scenarios—will produce much stronger intuition.
