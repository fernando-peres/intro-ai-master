Yes. For **4 × 2-hour classes**, I would simplify the previous syllabus substantially. With executives who generally have low technical knowledge, the objective should be **executive intuition and decision-making**, not technical coverage.

I would organize the course around one progression:

**Understand AI → Understand what became possible → Recognize opportunities → Design and defend an AI initiative**

And every class should repeat the same learning cycle:

**Concepts → examples → discussion → project application**

This makes the individual project the backbone of the discipline rather than something added at the end.

## Course: AI & Generative AI for Executives

### Overall learning objective

By the end of the course, participants should be able to:

* Understand the essential concepts behind AI, ML and Generative AI.
* Understand what changed with foundation models and multimodal AI.
* Recognize what AI can—and cannot—do.
* Identify valuable AI opportunities in their organization.
* Have productive conversations with technical teams and vendors.
* Ask the right questions about data, models, architecture, risk, cost and evaluation.
* Structure an AI initiative from business problem to pilot.
* Present and defend an AI project proposal.

I would explicitly say:

> **The objective is not to teach executives how to build AI. It is to teach them how to think about, identify, request, evaluate and manage AI initiatives.**

---

# Class 1 — Understanding AI and the GenAI Shift

### Central question

**What is AI, and what fundamentally changed with Generative AI?**

### Concepts — ~45 min

Start from familiar territory and progressively introduce GenAI.

**AI → Machine Learning → Deep Learning → Foundation Models → Generative AI**

Cover:

* What is AI?
* AI vs automation
* Traditional software vs Machine Learning
* Supervised/unsupervised learning at an intuitive level
* Training vs inference
* What is a model?
* What is Generative AI?
* Foundation models
* LLMs
* Transformers — intuition, not architecture details
* Tokens
* Prompts and context
* Why LLMs can generate language
* Why they hallucinate
* What changed after foundation models

A useful executive mental model:

```text
Traditional Software
Rules + Data → Output

Machine Learning
Data + Desired Outcomes → Learned Model → Prediction

Generative AI
Large-scale Data → Foundation Model
                     +
              Context / Instructions
                     ↓
          New Content / Reasoning
```

### Discussion — ~30 min

Use provocative but accessible questions:

* Is ChatGPT a database?
* Does an LLM "know" things?
* Why can the same model write an email, analyze a contract and generate code?
* When would traditional ML still be better than GenAI?
* What does hallucination mean for a business application?
* What changed economically when natural language became an interface to computation?

Use 2–3 real examples rather than dozens.

### Individual project — ~35 min

**Project Step 1 — Problem & Opportunity**

Each executive identifies a real problem or opportunity from their organization.

They produce:

```text
Organization / Business Context

Problem or Opportunity

Who experiences the problem?

How is it handled today?

Why does it matter?

What would improve if we solved it?

Where might AI help?

Initial hypothesis:
"We believe AI could ______
for ______
in order to improve ______."
```

End with 10 minutes of sharing and feedback.

# Class 2 — What AI Can Do: Models, Modalities and Capabilities

### Central question

**What can modern AI actually do, and how do I recognize where it can create value?**

This is where I would introduce **modalities**, but organize them around capabilities rather than technology.

### Concepts — ~40 min

#### Modalities

Modern models can work with:

* Text
* Images
* Audio / speech
* Video
* Code
* Structured data
* Multiple modalities simultaneously

Introduce examples of:

**Text → text**
**Text → image**
**Image → text**
**Speech → text → reasoning → speech**
**Text → code**
**Video → understanding**
**Multimodal → multimodal**

Then move to the more important abstraction:

### AI capabilities

```text
UNDERSTAND
     ↓
Extract
Classify
Search
Summarize
     ↓
GENERATE
     ↓
Text / Image / Audio / Video / Code
     ↓
REASON
     ↓
Analyze
Compare
Recommend
Plan
     ↓
ACT
     ↓
Tools
APIs
Systems
Workflows
```

Introduce **embeddings** here—not mathematically, but as the intuition behind semantic similarity, search, recommendations and retrieval.

### Discussion — ~30 min

Give groups business processes and ask:

> "What AI capabilities are actually required?"

For example:

**Customer service**

Could involve:

`Understand → Search → Reason → Generate → Act`

rather than simply:

> "We need a chatbot."

This distinction is extremely valuable for executives.

### Individual project — ~40 min

**Project Step 2 — AI Opportunity Design**

They extend the project:

```text
Business problem
        ↓
Users
        ↓
Current workflow
        ↓
AI capabilities required
        ↓
Modalities
        ↓
Expected AI-assisted workflow
        ↓
Expected business outcome
```

Require them to explicitly answer:

> **Why AI?**

and:

> **Why GenAI rather than traditional software or traditional ML?**

This prevents the project from becoming "let's put ChatGPT into X."

# Class 3 — From AI Models to AI Systems

### Central question

**How does an AI idea become a real business system?**

This is probably the most important class for developing executive intuition.

Executives should understand that:

> **LLM ≠ AI solution.**

### Concepts — ~45 min

Introduce a simplified modern GenAI architecture:

```text
                  USER
                    │
                    ▼
             AI APPLICATION
                    │
        ┌───────────┼───────────┐
        │           │           │
     CONTEXT      MODEL       TOOLS
        │           │           │
   ┌────┼────┐      │      ┌────┼────┐
   │    │    │      │      │    │    │
 Data  RAG Memory   LLM    API  DB  Systems
        │
   Knowledge
```

Explain intuitively:

* Prompting
* Context engineering
* Context windows
* Embeddings
* Semantic search
* RAG
* Memory
* Tool/function calling
* Agents
* Agentic workflows
* Human-in-the-loop
* Model vs application
* AI orchestration / harnesses
* Evaluation
* Observability

I would introduce the progression:

```text
LLM
 ↓
LLM + Context
 ↓
LLM + Enterprise Knowledge (RAG)
 ↓
LLM + Tools
 ↓
AI Workflow
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Agentic System
```

This is much easier for executives to retain than teaching each technology independently.

### Discussion — ~25 min

Present a hypothetical vendor pitch:

> "Our AI agent will automate 80% of your customer service."

Ask the executives:

**What questions would you ask before approving the project?**

Guide the conversation toward:

* What data?
* Which model?
* What does the agent have access to?
* What can it actually do?
* How is quality measured?
* What happens when it's wrong?
* When does a human intervene?
* How is sensitive information handled?
* What does it cost?
* How will it integrate with existing systems?

Now the technical concepts become **management tools**.

### Individual project — ~40 min

**Project Step 3 — Solution Design**

No detailed technical architecture required.

They create a conceptual solution:

```text
USER
  ↓
AI EXPERIENCE
  ↓
AI CAPABILITIES
  ↓
CONTEXT / KNOWLEDGE / DATA
  ↓
MODEL
  ↓
TOOLS / ENTERPRISE SYSTEMS
  ↓
ACTION
  ↓
HUMAN OVERSIGHT
```

And answer:

* What information does the AI need?
* What systems must it access?
* What decisions can it make?
* What actions can it take?
* Where must a human remain involved?
* What could go wrong?

# Class 4 — From AI Opportunity to AI Initiative

### Central question

**How should an executive evaluate, launch and manage an AI initiative?**

Now shift from technology to **leadership and execution**.

### Concepts — ~35 min

Cover the AI project lifecycle:

```text
Opportunity
    ↓
Hypothesis
    ↓
Prototype
    ↓
Evaluation
    ↓
Pilot
    ↓
Production
    ↓
Monitoring
    ↓
Continuous Improvement
```

Important executive concepts:

* Experiment vs production system
* PoC vs prototype vs MVP
* AI evaluation
* Accuracy vs usefulness
* Reliability
* Hallucination
* Guardrails
* Human oversight
* Privacy and security
* Responsible AI / governance
* Cost at scale
* Build vs buy
* Vendor evaluation
* ROI
* Adoption and change management

A particularly important idea:

> **A successful AI demo proves possibility. It does not prove reliability, scalability, ROI or production readiness.**

### Discussion — ~25 min

Give them three proposals:

**A.** "Let's deploy ChatGPT to everyone."

**B.** "Let's build an AI agent that replaces the entire finance department."

**C.** "Let's use AI to reduce contract review time from 3 hours to 30 minutes, initially with mandatory human approval."

Ask them to identify what information they would need before approving each initiative.

The objective isn't to find the "correct" project. It is to practice **executive questioning**.

### Individual project — ~30 min

**Project Step 4 — AI Initiative Proposal**

Participants complete:

```text
1. Business problem

2. Target users

3. Current process

4. Proposed AI-enabled process

5. AI capabilities required

6. Data / knowledge required

7. Conceptual solution

8. Human role

9. Risks & guardrails

10. Success metrics

11. Pilot / MVP

12. Expected business impact
```

### Final presentation / defense — ~30 min

Each participant gives a short **3–5 minute executive pitch**.

Not:

> "Here is the AI technology I want to use."

But:

> **"Here is the business problem, why AI changes what is possible, how I propose using it, how we will test it, what could go wrong, and how we will measure success."**

## I would also add one recurring element: "Executive AI Intuition"

At the end of every concept, introduce an **Executive Takeaway** and an **Executive Question**.

For example:

**Concept: LLMs are probabilistic**

Takeaway:

> The same input does not necessarily guarantee exactly the same output, and fluent output does not guarantee correctness.

Executive question:

> **"How are we measuring whether the outputs are good enough for this business process?"**

**Concept: RAG**

Takeaway:

> The model doesn't necessarily need to be retrained to use proprietary organizational knowledge.

Executive question:

> **"Where does the system obtain the information needed to answer this question?"**

**Concept: Agents**

Takeaway:

> Giving an AI the ability to act creates more value, but also introduces more operational risk.

Executive question:

> **"What is this agent authorized to do without human approval?"**

This is probably the most important pedagogical mechanism in the course because you're translating **technical understanding into management behavior**.

### Final structure

The four classes then have a very clean narrative:

**Class 1 — UNDERSTAND**
*What is AI and what changed with GenAI?*

**Class 2 — IMAGINE**
*What can AI do, and where could it create value?*

**Class 3 — DESIGN**
*How do models, data, context, tools, agents and humans become an AI system?*

**Class 4 — LEAD**
*How do I evaluate, fund, pilot, govern and manage an AI initiative?*

That gives the discipline a stronger executive identity than trying to compress a conventional AI/ML syllabus into eight hours.
