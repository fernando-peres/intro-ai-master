I would make this a **core concept of Class 2 — IMAGINE**, because it prevents executives from leaving the capabilities class with the impression that “if the model can do it in a demo, we can deploy it.”

It also creates the perfect bridge from **Class 2 (possibilities)** to **Class 3 (engineering reliable systems)**.

## Class 2 — IMAGINE

### What can AI do — and what does it take to turn capability into value?

After modalities and capabilities, add a section:

## **From AI Magic to AI Engineering**

Start with the apparent contradiction:

> **Prototyping with GenAI has become remarkably easy. Building reliable AI systems for a real organization is still hard.**

Today someone can create an impressive prototype in minutes:

```text
Idea
 ↓
Prompt
 ↓
LLM
 ↓
Impressive Demo
```

This is genuinely important. GenAI has dramatically lowered the cost of experimentation.

But then introduce the distinction:

```text
                    EASY
                     │
Idea → Prompt → Prototype → Demo
                     │
                     │
              ───────┼───────
                     │
                     ▼
                Production
                     │
       ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
       │             │             │
    Reliable      Secure       Integrated
       │             │             │
    Evaluated    Governed      Observable
       │             │             │
    Scalable     Controlled     Maintainable

                    HARDER
```

The executive intuition:

> **A prototype proves that something may be possible. It does not prove that it will work reliably inside your organization.**

## The “One Prompt” Myth

I would explicitly address what executives see on LinkedIn, X, YouTube, conferences, vendor demos, etc.

Not dismissively—the demos are often real. The **conclusion drawn from them is often the problem**.

For example:

> “I built a customer-service agent with one prompt in 10 minutes.”

Perhaps they did.

But ask:

**Does it know your products?**

**Does it know today's prices?**

**Can it identify the customer?**

**Can it access their orders?**

**Can it distinguish confidential from public information?**

**Does it know what it is authorized to refund?**

**What happens when it's wrong?**

**Can you measure its error rate?**

**Can you reproduce and investigate failures?**

**Can 100,000 customers use it?**

**What does it cost at that scale?**

Suddenly:

```text
"One Prompt"

      ↓

Prompt
+ Context
+ Company Knowledge
+ Customer Data
+ Business Rules
+ APIs
+ Permissions
+ Workflow
+ Memory
+ Evaluation
+ Guardrails
+ Monitoring
+ Human Escalation

      ↓

Production AI System
```

This is a very powerful reveal for executives.

# Demo ≠ Product ≠ Production System

I'd make this one of the central frameworks of the entire course.

### Demo

**“Can AI do this?”**

Quick prompt, manually selected examples, limited users.

### Prototype

**“Can we make this work for our use case?”**

Realistic data, initial workflow, early integration.

### Product

**“Does this solve a real user problem?”**

UX, integration, feedback, business process, adoption.

### Production AI System

**“Can we depend on this?”**

Reliability, security, evaluation, monitoring, scale, cost, governance, support.

So:

```text
DEMO
"Wow, it works!"

   ↓

PROTOTYPE
"It works on realistic cases."

   ↓

PRODUCT
"People actually want/use it."

   ↓

PRODUCTION
"We can depend on it."
```

That last transition is especially important.

# Teach them to recognize “AI Magic”

Rather than telling executives to distrust social media, give them a **mental checklist**.

Whenever they see:

> “AI can now do X.”

Train them to ask:

### 1. Capability

**Can the model perform the task at all?**

### 2. Reliability

**How often does it perform it correctly?**

### 3. Context

**Does it work with our data, terminology and business rules?**

### 4. Integration

**Can it work with our actual systems and processes?**

### 5. Scale

**Does it still work with thousands/millions of requests?**

### 6. Risk

**What happens when it is wrong?**

### 7. Economics

**Is the value greater than the total cost of operating it?**

This turns skepticism into **executive due diligence**, rather than “AI is hype.”

# An excellent Class 2 exercise

I would actually show them a very impressive AI demo.

For example:

> “An AI agent receives a customer complaint, analyzes it, checks the order and automatically issues a refund.”

Then ask:

> **“Would you deploy this tomorrow?”**

Initially some will probably say yes.

Then progressively reveal constraints:

```text
Customer asks:
"I didn't receive my €2,000 order.
Please refund me."

Agent:
"Of course. I've processed your refund."
```

Now ask:

> How did it authenticate the customer?

> How did it know whether the order was delivered?

> Who authorized it to refund €2,000?

> What if the customer has done this five times?

> What if the CRM is unavailable?

> What if the model calls the wrong API?

> What evidence should we retain?

> At what value should a human approve the refund?

You can transform:

> **“Wow, AI can do this.”**

into:

> **“What system would we need around the AI to safely do this?”**

And that's exactly the intellectual transition into Class 3.

# It improves the four-class narrative

I would now frame the whole course around four increasingly sophisticated questions.

### Class 1 — UNDERSTAND

**What is AI, what changed with GenAI, and what are its limitations?**

> *GenAI is powerful but imperfect.*

### Class 2 — IMAGINE

**What can we do with it—and how do we separate capability from hype?**

> *Prototyping is cheap. Reliable business systems are not.*

Students learn modalities, capabilities, opportunity discovery, **demo vs prototype vs production**, and how to critically evaluate “AI magic.”

### Class 3 — DESIGN

**What do we need around the model to make it useful and reliable?**

> *AI value comes from the system, not just the model.*

Context engineering, RAG, memory, workflows, agents, tools, harnesses, evals, guardrails, observability, human-in-the-loop.

### Class 4 — LEAD

**How do we decide what to invest in and manage AI in the organization?**

> *Move from technological possibility to measurable organizational value.*

Strategy, prioritization, build/buy, governance, risk, ROI, organizational change, pilot → production → scale.

There is also a useful phrase you could repeat throughout the four classes:

> **“Possible is not the same as reliable. Reliable is not the same as valuable.”**

That captures a large part of the executive intuition you're trying to develop.
